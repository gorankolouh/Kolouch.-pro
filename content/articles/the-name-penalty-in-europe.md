A CV lands on a recruiter's desk. Same degree, same experience, same cover letter — but the name at the top reads "Habiba Mahmoud" instead of "Lukas Becker." Across Europe, that single line of text is often enough to decide whether the phone ever rings. Call it the name penalty. The evidence for it is no longer anecdotal — it is documented, and in several cases, funded by the very governments whose institutions it implicates.

## Germany: a 46-point response gap

A 2025 correspondence study out of Siegen University sent more than 50,000 near-identical applications to German employers and institutions under different names. The fictitious "Lukas Becker" received a reply 67% of the time. "Habiba Mahmoud," an Arabic-sounding name, fared worst — drawing replies to only 36 of every 100 applications — while "Yusuf Kaya," a Turkish name, received 52 replies. That is not statistical noise. It is a structural pattern in which applicants with Muslim or non-European names are treated as a separate, disadvantaged tier, regardless of qualifications.

## France: the government tested itself

France has gone further than most EU states in measuring this directly. In 2020, a government-commissioned correspondence study — conducted through DARES, the labour ministry's statistics service — sent more than 10,000 applications to 40 of the country's largest companies. The results confirmed the same pattern found in Germany: candidates with names signaling North African or Muslim origin were called back at meaningfully lower rates than identically qualified candidates with names read as ethnically French.

## The Netherlands: when a name becomes a fraud flag

Name-based discrimination is not confined to hiring. In the Dutch childcare benefits scandal — the *toeslagenaffaire* — a tax authority algorithm used dual nationality and name-based signals as fraud-risk variables, disproportionately flagging families with non-Dutch-sounding names for aggressive, often baseless, fraud investigations. The Dutch Parliamentary Inquiry, published under the title "Ongekend Onrecht" ("Unprecedented Injustice"), found that roughly 26,000 families were wrongly accused, with devastating financial and personal consequences. The scandal brought down the Dutch government in a resignation in 2021 — a rare instance in which algorithmic, name-linked discrimination produced direct political accountability.

## Housing and the wider pattern

The same dynamic reproduces itself in the rental market. Cross-country audit studies of housing discrimination — including meta-analyses compiled by the OECD — have repeatedly found that applicants with Muslim-sounding or non-European names receive fewer callbacks and viewings from landlords than identically qualified applicants with domestic-sounding names, independent of income or employment status.

At the EU level, the Fundamental Rights Agency's 2024 survey "Being Muslim in the EU" corroborates the pattern at scale: Muslim respondents across member states report discrimination in employment, housing, and public services at rates far higher than the general population — with many identifying their name, rather than any interaction or interview, as the point where they were filtered out.

## Why this matters for enforcement

EU anti-discrimination law already prohibits exactly this kind of treatment. The gap is not in the text of the law — it is in the practical ability of an applicant to ever find out why their CV, tenancy application, or benefits file was rejected. A name-based filter leaves no paper trail. That is precisely the "access to rights" problem this research is meant to surface: protections that exist on paper, and the practical difficulty of a rejected applicant ever proving why the door didn't open.
