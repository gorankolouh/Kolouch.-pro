Nearly one in two Muslims across 13 EU member states has experienced racial discrimination — a sharp rise from 2016, according to the EU's own Fundamental Rights Agency. Employment. Housing. Policing. Education. Every domain of ordinary life. In the same period, several governments have moved to restrict what Muslims may wear in public, what they may say in prayer, and which organisations they may join — official state policy, in countries that describe themselves as liberal democracies and signatories to every major human rights instrument in existence.

At the same time, the most powerful media platform on earth is owned and personally operated by a man who has repeatedly used it to amplify anti-Muslim narratives to hundreds of millions of people — at a scale no parliament, minister, or policy could match.

This briefing does four things. First, it documents the scale of discrimination with primary data. Second, it catalogues specific, named, recent incidents of state action and social hostility. Third, it explains in precise legal terms why those actions are wrong — and where the legal pathway for challenge is strong, and where it is not. Fourth, it examines the role of platform X in amplifying this crisis.

The law exists. The rights exist. What is missing, consistently, is access, coordination, and the institutional will to use them.

## Part I — The Scale of the Problem: Primary Data

The most authoritative recent data comes from the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA), whose October 2024 report *Being Muslim in the EU* surveyed 9,604 self-identified Muslims across Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Spain, and Sweden.

### What the numbers show

- 47% reported racial discrimination in the five years before the survey — up from 39% in 2016. In Austria the figure was 71%, in Germany 68%, in Finland 63%.
- 39% reported discrimination when looking for work; 35% while in employment — both up sharply from 2016.
- 35% reported discrimination in housing — up from 22% in 2016.
- 27% reported racist harassment at least once in the prior five years; most reported it more than once.
- 4% reported a physical racist attack in the prior five years.
- 49% of those stopped by police believed the stop was ethnically or religiously motivated — up from 42% in 2016.
- Muslim respondents were three times more likely to leave school early than the general EU population (30% vs. 9.6%), and twice as likely to live in overcrowded housing.
- Only 6% of those who felt discriminated against filed any complaint. 68% of victims of racist violence never reported it — most commonly because they expected nothing would change.

*"A worrying surge in racism and discrimination against Muslims in Europe."* — FRA Director Sirpa Rautio, October 2024

Critically, this survey data was collected in 2021–2022 — before October 2023 and the subsequent Gaza war. Independent researchers and civil-society monitors document a further deterioration since then. The numbers above are a floor, not a ceiling.

### The underreporting crisis — a legal problem

The 6% complaint rate is not merely a social statistic. It is a systemic legal failure. In EU law, the enforcement of non-discrimination directives depends heavily on individuals filing complaints — the mechanism for collective or structural enforcement is severely limited. A legal architecture designed around individual complaint is functionally constrained when 94% of victims do not complain.

## Part II — Documented Incidents and State Actions (2024–2026)

Statistics establish the pattern. Individual cases establish the reality. The following are verified, sourced incidents — not speculation or allegation. Each is legally significant, as explained below.

### The face-covering bans — state action against religious expression

**Switzerland: first fine under the burqa ban, March 2025.** Switzerland's nationwide ban on facial coverings in public entered into force on 1 January 2025, following a 2021 referendum that passed by a margin of 51.2%. In March 2025, the first fine was issued in Zurich — CHF 100, against a woman covering her face for religious reasons. She refused to pay. Switzerland joins France, Belgium, Austria, Bulgaria, Denmark, and parts of Italy and Spain in criminalising a garment worn almost exclusively by Muslim women.

The legal issue is not whether governments may regulate public space — they may. The issue is whether a regulation whose primary and demonstrable real-world effect falls on a single religious group can be justified by a goal as vague as "living together" or "social cohesion." The burden of justification required under Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights, read with Article 14, is demanding — and most of these laws have never faced serious proportionality scrutiny at the domestic level. A law neutral on its face but discriminatory in effect — disparate-impact discrimination — is as problematic under EU equality law (Race Equality Directive 2000/43/EC, Employment Equality Directive 2000/78/EC) as direct discrimination.

**France — the ongoing laboratory.** France's face-covering ban has been in force since 2011. Between 2011 and 2026, over 4,000 women have been fined under it. In 2023–2024, the government extended the prohibition to school sports and swimming pools through administrative guidance — a measure not based in statute and therefore of questionable legality. France also banned the abaya in state schools in September 2023, citing laïcité; the Conseil d'État upheld the measure without full proportionality analysis. France's ICCPR Article 18 obligations require rigorous necessity and proportionality analysis of restrictions on religious dress — a bar France has not clearly cleared.

### Restricting the call to prayer — Denmark, 2026

In June 2026, Danish Immigration Minister Morten Bødskov announced a renewed government investigation into a nationwide ban on the Adhan — the third such attempt since 2020 — stating the call to prayer should not be heard "over Danish rooftops," framing the measure as resistance to "Islamisation." No other religious sound — church bells, which ring in every Danish town multiple times daily — is subject to equivalent restriction.

A law that prohibits the Adhan while permitting church bells is not a neutral noise regulation. The comparator is not "all loud sound" but "equivalent religious practice," and church bells are the equivalent religious practice. The discriminatory intent is explicit in the minister's own framing.

### The Dutch Muslim Brotherhood motion — targeting an organisation

On 17 March 2026, the Dutch House of Representatives adopted, by 76 votes, a motion calling on the government to ban the Muslim Brotherhood and its affiliated organisations. The Dutch intelligence service (AIVD) has explicitly stated that the Muslim Brotherhood does not exist in the Netherlands as a structured organisation capable of being banned under current law. The motion proceeds on the basis of association with a name and a perceived religious-political identity, not a documented security threat. Where a state's own security service finds no structured organisation presenting a domestic threat, the "pressing social need" threshold Article 11 ECHR requires cannot easily be met.

### Quran burning — when free expression is used as a weapon

- 17 February 2024, Stockholm: public Quran burning, no prosecution.
- 30 January 2025: Salwan Momika, the central figure in Sweden's 2023 wave of Quran burnings, was shot and killed in Stockholm.
- 13 February 2025, London: a man burning a Quran outside the Turkish consulate was attacked with a knife; both men were prosecuted.
- November 2024: activist Rasmus Paludan was sentenced to four months in prison in Sweden — not for burning the Quran, which remains lawful, but for accompanying hate speech.

The pattern across jurisdictions is consistent: burning a religious text is protected as free expression; only accompanying speech that incites hatred is prosecutable, and such prosecutions remain rare and inconsistent. Article 20(2) of the ICCPR requires states to prohibit advocacy of religious hatred that constitutes incitement — a mandatory, not discretionary, obligation. States that repeatedly permit public Quran burnings without prosecution arguably fall short of that positive obligation.

### Employment, housing, and policing — the daily reality

Two cases are especially instructive. In *IX v. WABE* (CJEU, C-804/18, 2021), the Court held an employer may prohibit religious symbols including the headscarf only under a genuinely neutral policy applied to all religious and political symbols, justified by a legitimate aim beyond mere customer preference — a ruling frequently misapplied by employers who ban headscarves without such a policy. In *Basu v. Germany* (ECtHR, 2023), the Court found a violation of Article 14 read with Article 8 where Germany failed to adequately investigate a complaint that an identity check was racially motivated — establishing that a state's failure to investigate a discrimination complaint is itself a human rights violation, directly relevant to the 49% of European Muslims who report ethnically motivated police stops.

## Part III — Platform X and the Amplification of Islamophobia

This section is not about offensive posts. It is about the systematic use of the most powerful private communications infrastructure in the world to amplify narratives of Muslim threat, dangerousness, and civilisational incompatibility, at a speed and scale no government propaganda apparatus in history has matched.

Elon Musk's personal X account has approximately 200 million followers as of June 2026. He is the platform's majority shareholder, CEO, and the person who personally sets its content moderation policy. The European Commission has formally found that he exercises "decisive influence and effective control" over the platform — a finding made in the context of Digital Services Act enforcement, which classifies X as a Very Large Online Platform subject to its strictest obligations.

**August 2024 — the Southport response.** Musk shared a meme widely characterised as Islamophobic, then, days later, three children were murdered in Southport. False claims spread rapidly on X that the attacker was a Muslim asylum seeker; he was not. By the time the truth was established, mosques were being attacked across England. Musk wrote "Civil war is inevitable." Amnesty International's subsequent review documented that in the two weeks following the attack, a previously banned far-right account reinstated after Musk's acquisition received over 580 million views.

**January 2025 — the grooming gang campaign: 1.53 billion views.** Musk published 51 posts over several days reframing historical UK "grooming gang" cases — extensively investigated and prosecuted — as a collective cultural and religious indictment of British Pakistani Muslim men. An independent study by the Center for the Study of Organized Hate found the posts generated approximately 1.2 billion engagements, a roughly 93-fold increase over equivalent 2024 discourse; total reach hit 1.53 billion views; and 53.81% of sampled posts during the peak period actively promoted hatred against Muslims, British Pakistani men, and South Asians as a collective group.

**What changed on the platform itself.** Since 2022, X has dismantled most of its Trust and Safety function, disbanded its external safety advisory council, reinstated thousands of previously banned accounts (including some banned specifically for anti-Muslim hate speech), and — per Amnesty International's review of its own published recommender-system code — configured its algorithm to prioritise engagement regardless of accuracy or harm. A year after the Southport riots, the Centre for Countering Digital Hate found X was still failing to remove content explicitly violating its own published rules.

**The legal dimension.** The European Commission fined X €120 million in December 2025 — for deceptive verification design and advertising transparency, not for its handling of anti-Muslim hate speech. That is a live gap in the accountability framework: the DSA's systemic-risk provisions (Article 34) require Very Large Online Platforms to assess and mitigate risks to fundamental rights, including freedom of religion and non-discrimination. X's documented pattern of failing to remove hate speech that violates its own rules, combined with algorithmic amplification of that content, plausibly constitutes a failure to mitigate a systemic risk under Article 34. No regulator has yet pursued that specific argument — a case for it is set out in a separate piece, *The Fine That Missed the Point*.

## Part IV — Where the Law Is on the Side of Muslim Communities

This section does not argue every state action described above is illegal — the ECtHR has upheld face-covering bans, and the legal landscape is genuinely mixed. It sets out, with specificity, where the law is strongest.

**The constitutional architecture.** Every EU member state has ratified the European Convention on Human Rights (Arts. 9, 10, 11, 14), the ICCPR (Arts. 18, 19, 20(2), 26), and the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (Arts. 10, 11, 12, 20, 21). These are binding legal obligations, directly enforceable before domestic courts, the CJEU, and the ECtHR.

**The proportionality principle.** Under the ECHR, any restriction on a Convention right must be prescribed by law, pursue a legitimate aim, and be necessary in a democratic society — meeting a pressing social need and proportionate to that aim. This is where most of the measures above are weakest: a ban on the Adhan with no equivalent restriction on church bells is not the minimum necessary measure; a motion to ban an organisation the state's own intelligence service says does not exist cannot meet a pressing social need; an employer's headscarf ban justified by "customer preference" fails CJEU proportionality standards. The burden of justification sits with the state, not the individual — a point consistently obscured in public debate and consistently relevant in litigation.

**The positive obligation.** European human rights law also requires states to protect rights from interference by third parties. *Basu v. Germany* and *R.B. v. Hungary* (ECtHR, 2016, on failure to investigate an anti-Roma hate crime) establish that failure to investigate is itself a violation — a framework especially powerful for platform regulation. A state that knows a Very Large Online Platform is systematically amplifying incitement against a religious community, and takes no DSA enforcement action, may itself be in breach of a positive obligation to protect that community.

**Indirect discrimination.** EU equality law prohibits not only direct discrimination but neutral rules that disadvantage one religious group without objective justification. A face-covering ban is facially neutral but falls almost exclusively on Muslim women — a textbook indirect-discrimination case requiring the state to justify it, not the other way around. The same framework applies to funding restrictions that name "foreign" sources but exempt Catholic dioceses receiving Vatican support, and to dress-code employment policies that disproportionately exclude Muslim candidates.

## Part V — The Underused Legal Tool: the DSA

The Digital Services Act, in full application for Very Large Online Platforms since August 2023, requires them under Article 34 to identify and assess systemic risks — explicitly including risks to freedom of religion and non-discrimination — and under Article 35 to implement reasonable mitigation measures. The Commission's December 2025 enforcement action against X addressed deceptive design and advertising transparency; it did not address Articles 34–35.

The argument that has not yet been formally made: X's documented pattern of algorithmic amplification of anti-Muslim content, combined with its systematic failure to enforce its own hate speech rules against accounts that explicitly violate them, constitutes a failure to adequately assess and mitigate systemic risks to the fundamental right of non-discrimination on grounds of religion. This is a live, untested, and legally serious argument under Articles 34–35 DSA.

## Conclusion

Being Muslim in Europe today means, on the documented evidence above, living a measurably harder life than being non-Muslim in the same countries — harder in finding work, in finding housing, in dealings with the police, in the simple practice of one's faith, and in navigating a digital environment that has become, for many, a space of daily hostility.

The law that governs this continent was written, in the wreckage of the twentieth century, around a different commitment: human dignity, equality before the law, and the protection of every person's right to live without fear of who they are. That commitment has legal force — statute, treaty, and binding obligation, not sentiment. The evidence in this briefing is evidence that the gap between what the law promises and what it delivers, for millions of European Muslims, remains wide.

The moment a legal order treats one group's dignity as negotiable, it has made everyone's dignity negotiable. The law does not permit this. Neither should the institutions responsible for applying it.
