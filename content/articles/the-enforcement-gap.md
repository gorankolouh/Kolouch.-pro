*Of everything I've read and cited in years of EU rights advocacy, the Digital Services Act is the text I keep coming back to — my favorite piece of legislation on the books. This article is written in that spirit. Not a takedown of the law. A demand that it actually gets enforced — in practice, not just in print.*

## A law with no shortage of ambition

On paper, Europe has the most advanced digital-safety framework in the world. The Digital Services Act (DSA) requires Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs — services with more than 45 million monthly EU users) to run annual systemic-risk assessments under Articles 34 and 35, covering everything from illegal content to harm to minors' physical and mental wellbeing. Article 28 goes further: "appropriate and proportionate measures to ensure a high level of privacy, safety and security" for any minor who can access the service — no qualifiers, no carve-outs. Add the GDPR, the AI Act, and the July 2025 Commission Guidelines on the protection of minors, and the EU looks, from a distance, like the jurisdiction that finally solved platform accountability.

The distance is the problem. Up close, the picture is a genuinely well-drafted law running on an enforcement engine that is, so far, radically undersized against the harm it exists to stop.

## Who has actually been fined — and who hasn't

Start with a correction that matters, because it's the single most common confusion in this debate: the two most-fined US tech giants in Europe, Google and Meta, are mostly being punished under a different law than the one this article is about.

Google's big EU fines are antitrust and Digital Markets Act (DMA) fines — not DSA fines. In July 2026, the EU's top court permanently upheld the Commission's €4.1 billion Android antitrust fine, closing a case that started in 2015. Separately, Brussels is reportedly finishing a DMA decision against Google Search for favoring its own services in results — a case that, given the DMA's cap of 10% of global turnover, could clear $35 billion and land before the Commission's August 2026 recess. Google has also previously been fined €2.95 billion over its ad-tech business. All three are competition-law cases, run by a different Commission department (DG COMP, not DG CONNECT) under a different statute with a different logic: market dominance, not platform safety.

Under the DSA specifically, the entire fine record is two decisions:

- X — €120 million (December 2025): the DSA's first-ever fine, after a two-year investigation, for the deceptive "blue checkmark" verification system, an opaque advertising repository, and blocked researcher access to public data.
- Temu — €200 million (28 May 2026): the DSA's largest fine to date, for failing to properly assess and mitigate the risk of illegal and dangerous products on the platform (Articles 34–35).

That is the complete list. Two fines, roughly eighteen months into active enforcement, against a law that covers every VLOP operating in a market of 450 million people.

Google, under the DSA, currently has no fine — only a fresh, unresolved complaint. On 21 May 2026, BEUC (the European Consumer Organisation) and 29 national consumer groups filed formal DSA complaints against Google, Meta, and TikTok — not over child safety this time, but financial scam advertising. Between December 2025 and March 2026, the groups reported roughly 900 suspected fraudulent ads directly to the platforms; only 27% were removed, and more than half the reports were rejected outright or simply never answered. TikTok removed just 21% of what it was shown, Meta 43%, Google 60% — meaning even the best performer in the complaint left nearly two in five reported scam ads running. Independent reporting has put Meta's annual scam-ad revenue in the billions. The complaint asks the Commission and national Digital Services Coordinators (DSCs) to open formal Article 34–35 investigations and, if warranted, impose fines of up to 6% of global turnover — the DSA's statutory ceiling. As of this writing: no investigation has formally opened. It is a complaint, not yet a case.

## The rest of the open docket

Beyond X, Temu, and the scam-ad complaint, the DSA's minor-protection enforcement pipeline currently includes:

- Meta / Instagram & Facebook — investigation opened April 2026 into whether Meta failed to identify, assess, and mitigate the risk of under-13s accessing its platforms, and failed to remove them once there. Meta disputes the preliminary findings. Still open.
- Four adult-content platforms — preliminary findings issued 26 March 2026 for failing to keep minors out, in breach of Article 28. Still open.
- TikTok — the European Board for Digital Services reviewed Commission preliminary findings on TikTok's minor-protection compliance as of its April 2026 meeting. Still open.
- Roblox (Netherlands) — the Dutch DSC (ACM) opened an investigation on 30 January 2026 into whether Roblox adequately protects minors from violent content and harmful interactions. Expected to take roughly 12 months.
- Snapchat (Netherlands → EU-level) — opened by the ACM in September 2025 over failure to prevent illegal vape sales to minors; folded into a broader Commission-level case as of 26 March 2026. Still open.
- Shein — investigation opened 17 February 2026, covering illegal products (including, notably, childlike sex dolls), addictive design features, and recommender-system transparency. Still open.

Every one of these, apart from the two fines already issued, remains unresolved as of mid-2026. Some were opened in the first half of 2024 — meaning cases central to child protection are now approaching or past the two-year mark with no public resolution, while the platforms under investigation keep operating exactly as before.

## Enforcement without a victory lap

One detail says as much about the enforcement gap as the fine numbers themselves. When the Commission announced its first-ever DSA fine, it did so through a routine press release on a Friday afternoon — no joint appearance by the Commission President or the responsible Executive Vice-President — and it initially declined to publish the full legal decision, citing, per its own stated reasoning as reported by Tech Policy Press, the risk that disclosure could discourage companies from cooperating in future cases. Civil society groups who had spent two years supplying evidence for that case noted the contrast with how loudly Brussels publicizes its antitrust wins against the same companies. The DSA's first real enforcement action landed publicly less like a milestone and more like something to get through quietly — and that is its own signal to every platform still under investigation about how much political weight actually stands behind the law.

## The actual mechanism of failure — and why it's fixable

A recent academic mapping of DSA minor-protection enforcement ("Mind the Gap," Interface-EU) traces the failure to three specific points, none of which is "the text is too weak":

1. **Platforms exploit legal ambiguity.** Many rely on "performance of a contract" as their GDPR legal basis for processing minors' data — without checking whether the user has the legal capacity to enter a contract under national law in the first place. The obligation to check was always there. Nobody enforced it.
2. **"Age assurance" in practice still means self-declaration.** The Commission's own preliminary findings against Meta note that compliance has largely rested on users typing in a birthdate — precisely the gap that let under-13s onto a platform legally required to keep them out.
3. **Digital Services Coordinators lack resources, clear mandate, or, in the paper's own words, the will to sanction.** Twenty-seven member states are meant to enforce one harmonised law with wildly uneven staffing, technical expertise, and political appetite for confronting companies that are also major advertisers and employers in their own economies.

What's easy to miss in all of this: the DSA already contains tools built for exactly this bottleneck, and they are barely being used.

## Out of the box: tools that already exist and aren't being touched

None of what follows requires new legislation. It requires the Commission and national DSCs to use powers the DSA already gives them.

1. **Use Article 70 interim measures — now, not after the investigation ends.** Buried in the DSA's enforcement chapter is a provision allowing the Commission to order interim measures against a platform mid-investigation, "where there is urgency due to the risk of serious damage for the recipients of the service," based on nothing more than a prima facie finding of infringement. This is precisely built for a case like Roblox or Snapchat: instead of waiting a year or two for a final ruling while children remain exposed, the Commission could order specific interim safeguards the moment the prima facie evidence is strong enough. It has essentially never been used in the child-safety cases open right now.
2. **Turn the fine into a clock, not a lump sum, using Article 76.** The DSA already allows periodic penalty payments of up to 5% of a platform's average daily global turnover, accruing per day of continued non-compliance, on top of any lump-sum fine. A one-off €120 million fine against a company with tens of billions in revenue is an accounting entry. A daily-accruing penalty is a genuine incentive to move fast — and the legal basis is already sitting in the text, unused.
3. **Make enforcement self-funding.** DSA fines currently go into the EU's general budget. A structural fix: legislate that a fixed share of DSA fine revenue is ring-fenced to fund Digital Services Coordinator staffing and technical capacity across the 27 member states.
4. **Extend "trusted flagger" status to systemic-risk complaints, not just individual content reports.** The DSA already lets platforms fast-track content takedown requests from vetted "trusted flaggers" (Article 22). There is no equivalent fast lane for the kind of systemic, evidence-heavy complaint BEUC just filed against Google, Meta, and TikTok. Formally accrediting organisations like BEUC, EDRi, and national child-safety bodies as trusted systemic-risk flaggers — with a statutory clock requiring the Commission to open or formally decline an investigation within a fixed window — would convert months of silence into an auditable, public timeline.
5. **Borrow individual accountability from EU financial regulation.** Banking regulators already hold named senior executives personally responsible for specific risk domains, under frameworks modeled on the UK's Senior Managers Regime. There is no equivalent under the DSA. A "designated minors'-safety officer" requirement, with personal liability exposure, would change boardroom incentives in a way that corporate fines — treated as a cost of doing business — visibly haven't.

## The age verification wallet: further along than most people realize, and still unresolved

The most concrete EU attempt to close the gap without recreating a surveillance system is the age-verification "mini-wallet," and its status has moved fast in 2026:

- 14 July 2025 — Commission publishes the first technical blueprint.
- 15 April 2026 — Commission President von der Leyen declares the app "technically ready," built by Scytáles and T-Systems, explicitly comparing it to the EU's COVID digital certificate as a model for rapid, bloc-wide rollout.
- 29 April 2026 — Commission adopts a non-binding Recommendation urging member states to accelerate rollout, with implementation plans due 30 June 2026 and at least one compliant national solution live by 31 December 2026.
- Summer 2026 — the app is scheduled to reach app stores in pilot countries.
- By January 2027 — the mini-wallet is meant to be live in all 27 member states; after that point, the Commission has signaled that not offering an equivalent mechanism could itself be treated as a DSA compliance gap.

How it actually works: the system runs on zero-knowledge-proof cryptography. A government ID or passport is checked once by a qualified issuer, which signs a credential stored in a secure enclave on the phone. When a platform asks "are you over 18?", the device generates a mathematical proof that the hidden date of birth satisfies that condition — without transmitting the date of birth, a name, or a document number, and without the issuer ever seeing when or where the credential gets used. This is, on the merits, a materially better design than the "upload your ID" model spreading through UK and US law.

Who's building it: seven "front-runner" member states are piloting integration into national digital identity systems — France (France Identité), Denmark (MitID), Greece (Gov.gr Wallet), Italy (IO app / IT Wallet), Spain (Cartera Digital), Cyprus, and Ireland. The remaining twenty are expected to follow through 2026 into early 2027, though the Commission itself has acknowledged growing doubt that all will hit the December deadline.

Two open wounds in an otherwise well-engineered system:

1. A March 2026 independent security review of the app's open-source code found the issuer component has no way to confirm that passport verification actually happened on the user's own device rather than being spoofed remotely. Closing that gap would likely require sending more identifying passport data to the server — directly eroding the privacy guarantee the whole system is built around.
2. Ireland's pilot ties the age credential to citizens' PPS numbers — a national tax identification number — exactly the kind of durable, government-linked identifier that privacy advocates (EFF, European Digital Rights) warn turns a narrow "prove you're 18" tool into infrastructure that could later be repurposed to identify, not merely verify.

## What a fair opponent would say — and why the gap argument still holds

A serious privacy advocate would answer all of this with a genuine point: every one of the "out of the box" fixes above — interim measures, daily penalties, personal executive liability, a trusted-flagger fast lane — makes the Commission more powerful over platforms, and by extension over the speech and commerce that runs through them. A more muscular enforcement apparatus, even one built on existing legal text, is still a bigger lever for a future Commission with different priorities to pull. That risk doesn't disappear because today's proposals are well-intentioned.

The honest answer is that the risk is real and worth building safeguards against — judicial review stays intact under Article 81, decisions remain challengeable at the Court of Justice, and every mechanism above should carry sunset clauses and independent audit, not open-ended discretion. But the current alternative isn't some safer equilibrium. It's two fines, six-plus stalled investigations, and a scam-ad ecosystem reaching over 200 million Europeans a month while the tools to stop it sit unused in the statute book. Between a regulator that might one day overreach and platforms that are demonstrably, measurably under-supervised right now, Europe's actual choice in 2026 is not close.
